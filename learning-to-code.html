Why I'm Learning to Code as an Engineer

Exploring how programming and engineering can complement each other in the modern world.

Newton Wanyoike@asterisk58
A field note from the journey
POWERED BYCURIOSITY02 / 03
IN THIS ARTICLE Technology & engineeringThinking in systemsBuilding thingsAcross disciplinesStaying curious

More tools. More ways to solve problems.

At first, civil engineering and coding seemed like two completely different worlds to me. I associated civil engineering with structures, construction materials, surveying, and technical drawings, while coding seemed to revolve around programming languages, logic, algorithms, and computers.

But the more I explore both fields, the more I realise how much they have in common. One of the biggest connections is problem-solving. Both involve finding solutions to technical problems, often by breaking complex tasks into smaller, more manageable steps. That's one of the main reasons I'm learning to code.

01 Engineering is changing

Traditionally, civil engineering and computer science focused on different kinds of work. Engineers relied heavily on manual calculations, drafting technical drawings, and using analog methods to keep field measurement records and other data, while software developers focused on building digital tools and applications. Today, though, the two fields are increasingly connected. Modern engineering makes use of automation, computational modelling, and data-driven insights to help solve complex problems.

Building Information Modelling (BIM), for example, helps project teams coordinate different aspects of construction through shared digital models. Programming can also help engineers automate repetitive tasks. Python scripts, for instance, can process measurements, analyse data, or perform repeated calculations, potentially reducing hours of manual work to a much quicker process.

I'm still at the beginning of understanding how these applications work, but they make me curious about what's possible when engineering knowledge meets programming skills. There's a whole side of engineering I'd like to explore.

"I don't want to limit my curiosity to the boundaries of my course."

02 Learning to think in systems

One thing I find interesting about coding is how it encourages you to test ideas, learn from the results, and keep improving. You write code, run it, identify errors, and make changes. This cycle of testing and refinement is often called iteration.

I think this way of working has useful lessons for engineering, too. Large projects can be broken down into smaller components, individual problems can be investigated systematically, and different parts of a design can be checked before everything comes together. Of course, physical engineering projects have their own complexities, but the habit of thinking through a problem step by step is valuable in both fields.

When my code fails, I have to investigate instead of simply guessing. I check what went wrong, look for the cause, and try to understand why my solution didn't work. Debugging can be frustrating, but it's also teaching me patience, attention to detail, and the importance of questioning my own assumptions.

03 From learning code to building things

My interest in coding has led me into web development. Learning HTML, CSS, and JavaScript has helped me understand how websites are structured, styled, and made interactive. Building my personal portfolio has been one way of turning concepts I've learnt into something I can actually see, use, and improve.

There's something satisfying about starting with an idea, writing the code, running into errors, figuring them out, and gradually watching the project come together. It makes learning feel more practical because I'm not just reading about how things work; I'm actually trying to build them myself.

I'd also like to explore how digital skills could support my future engineering projects. That could mean presenting construction services online, creating simple tools to organise project information, or developing custom web and desktop applications for specific project needs.

These are possibilities I'm interested in exploring, not achievements I'm claiming to have already accomplished. For now, I'm building my skills one project at a time and learning more about what I might be able to do with them.

04 The advantage of learning across disciplines

I believe there's real value in learning to connect ideas from different fields. Sometimes, looking at a problem from another perspective helps you find an approach you might otherwise have missed.

An engineer who understands technology may find new ways to communicate designs, automate routine tasks, analyse project data, or improve the way information is organised. Coding doesn't automatically make someone a better engineer, of course, but it can expand the range of tools available to them.

At the same time, learning multiple disciplines takes time, and trying to learn everything at once can get overwhelming. There's a risk of spreading yourself too thin, collecting different skills without developing real competence in any of them.

That's something I have to keep in mind, too. My civil engineering studies and my ambition to design and build construction projects remain my priorities. Coding is an additional skill I'm developing alongside them, not an excuse to neglect the knowledge and experience I need to become a competent engineer.

05 Starting small and staying curious

I'm not an expert programmer, and I don't need to pretend to be one. I'm still learning, making mistakes, and figuring things out. For now, I'm focusing on understanding one concept at a time, building small projects, and improving through practice.

I want to become someone who can identify a problem, figure out what I need to learn, and work towards building a practical solution — whether that involves concrete, calculations, code, or a combination of all three.

For me, learning to code as a future civil engineer isn't about abandoning one path to follow another. It's about expanding my skills and exploring how the physical and digital worlds can work together. I'm still figuring out where that journey will lead, but that's part of what makes it exciting.

STAY CURIOUS. KEEP BUILDING.
