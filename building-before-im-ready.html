Building Before I'm Ready

Why starting small, experimenting and learning through mistakes matter more than waiting for perfection.

Newton Wanyoike@asterisk58
A field note from the journey

IN THIS ARTICLE The illusion of readySmall beginningsLearning through mistakesInvisible progressBuild, learn, repeat

Let preparation lead to practice.

There's a certain kind of hesitation that comes from wanting to do something well. You picture the perfect finished result, think about the skills you'll need, and start focusing on everything you still don't understand. This perfectionism makes you feel like you're never quite ready.

It's easy to tell yourself, "I just need to finish one more course, watch another tutorial, read one more textbook, or master one more framework before I start." Before you know it, preparing to begin becomes a project of its own.

You keep saying that you need a little more preparation. And somehow, the starting point keeps moving further away.

I've come to realise that waiting for the perfect moment can be procrastination in disguise. Sometimes, it's just a way of avoiding the mistakes, challenges, and experiences that would actually help me grow.

01 The illusion of being ready

When I started exploring web development, there was a lot I didn't know. HTML, CSS, and JavaScript kept introducing me to new concepts, and building a complete website felt like a much bigger task than simply following individual lessons.

I could have waited until I felt confident in every area before starting my first project. But I've learnt that some things only start making sense when you actually work on them. Building my first website exposed me to problems that tutorials hadn't prepared me for. I had to deal with unexpected errors, spend evenings debugging, and sometimes rebuild from scratch features that refused to work as expected.

Trust me it wasn't always smooth, but that's where a lot of the real learning happened.

“Sometimes, readiness is something you develop by starting.”

02 Small beginnings still count

It's easy to look at a small project and feel like it isn't much. A basic website. A simple program. A few pages of notes. A new habit you manage to practise for just ten minutes. None of these might seem impressive on their own.

But small beginnings give you room to practise. They turn ideas you've only read about into things you can actually work with, test, and improve. My personal portfolio, for example, gave me a chance to practise page structure, colours, layouts, navigation, and how to communicate ideas through design.

Was it perfect? Not at all. But it didn't need to be. I learnt a lot by simply building it, seeing what worked, and figuring out what needed to change.

That's something I'm learning to appreciate: a project doesn't have to be impressive to everyone else to be valuable to me.

03 Mistakes are part of the process

Mistakes give you feedback, sometimes immediately. When I get a calculation wrong in an assignment, during coursework, or while studying on my own, I try not to get too discouraged. Instead, I look at what went wrong. Was there a concept I hadn't understood properly? Did I skip a step or make an incorrect assumption?

These are the kinds of questions that help me spot gaps in my understanding. Sometimes, reading through a solution isn't enough. I need to attempt the problem myself, get something wrong, and work through the reasoning to understand where I went off track.

The same applies when I'm experimenting with code. A broken layout or a program that throws an error can be frustrating, but it also gives me something specific to investigate. I can check what happened, trace the problem to its cause, and try a better approach.

Of course, not every mistake is one you should make just to learn. Experimenting with a personal website is one thing; making unverified decisions about the structural safety of a building is another. Some mistakes are part of learning, while others need to be prevented through proper training, checking, and professional guidance.

The point isn't to stop worrying about mistakes altogether. It's to learn from the ones you can safely make and recognise when a situation calls for more knowledge, care, or supervision.

04 Progress is not always visible

It's easy to compare your first attempts with someone else's finished work. You see an impressive engineering project, a beautifully designed website, or someone who seems to know exactly what they're doing. What you don't always see are the years of practice, the failed attempts, and all the revisions that came before that final result.

I'm trying to remind myself that I don't have to compete with someone else's finished chapter while I'm still writing my first few pages. We're at different stages, learning different things, and working with different experiences. My job is to keep getting better at what I'm doing, not to rush just because someone else seems further ahead.

And progress doesn't always look the way you expect. Sometimes, it's finally completing a project you've been putting off. Sometimes, it's understanding a concept that confused you for days. Other times, it's recognising a weakness and deciding to work on it instead of pretending it isn't there.

These moments might not look like much from the outside, but they're still progress. Not every step forward needs an audience.

05 Build, learn, improve, repeat

I want to become a capable engineer, develop useful technical skills, build meaningful projects, and eventually create something of my own. Those are big ambitions, and I know they won't be achieved through motivation alone. They'll take practice, patience, and the discipline to keep learning even when things feel slow or progress isn't obvious.

I've also realised that completing a basic, imperfect version of a project can teach me things that months of passive studying might not. It gives me something real to work with, a clearer idea of what I understand, and a starting point for improvement. It also gives me the confidence to take on the next challenge.

So I'm learning to give myself permission to be a beginner. To build things that still need work. To ask questions when something doesn't make sense. To make mistakes, take responsibility for them, and come back with a better approach.

That doesn't mean rushing into things without thinking or pretending preparation doesn't matter. Preparation is important. But there's a difference between preparing yourself to begin and endlessly preparing because you're afraid to start.

Sometimes, you have to take what you know, work with what you have, and learn the rest along the way. You won't have every answer at the beginning, and that's okay. The important thing is to keep moving, stay curious, and be willing to improve.

START WHERE YOU ARE.

You don't have to feel ready to begin. Sometimes, beginning is how you become ready.
